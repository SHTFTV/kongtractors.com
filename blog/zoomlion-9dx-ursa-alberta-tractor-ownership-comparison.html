Zoomlion 9DX and Alberta’s Ursa: two answers to different tractor jobs

A source-checked comparison of Zoomlion’s claimed 1,600 hp peak hybrid 9DX and Ursa’s mechanical-diesel approach: scale, repair planning, support and the unanswered ownership questions.

Kong Tractors editorial / Industry Army Marketing · Published October 29, 2026

Read the status, not just the horsepower: Zoomlion figures are manufacturer claims. Ursa’s current website labels its specifications conceptual and prices samples. This is original analysis, not a matched equipment test, stock report or sales quote.

The headline is power. The decision is the work.

A 1,600-horsepower hybrid tractor and a mechanically focused Alberta lineup make a striking contrast. They do not form a like-for-like buying contest. The more useful comparison is between two approaches to getting work done: concentrating sophisticated power and control in a very large field machine, or keeping a smaller tractor’s mechanical layout central to the ownership proposition.

This original Kong comparison follows Future Farming’s October 28 report by Bob Karsten, then checks the machine identity against Zoomlion’s own October 26 announcement. The manufacturer names the new four-track model 9DX. Its separate English-language DX7004 announcement describes a different wheeled tractor with 1,200 hp peak output. Do not combine those specifications or treat either headline as an independently verified world record.

What is actually claimed for the 9DX?

Zoomlion’s October 26 Chinese-language release gives a peak output of 1,600 hp for the 9DX, an 800 V dual-motor system and hybrid continuously variable transmission. It also states 7,200 Nm peak torque, 40 km/h maximum speed, 636 L/min hydraulic flow and 762 mm tracks. These are manufacturer figures, not Kong measurements. The announcement positions the tractor for intensive work on very large farms.

Future Farming reports on the announcement but does not supply an independent field test. Its October 28 story says the engine identity and unladen weight had not been disclosed. Those details remain unverified in the source set consulted for this article. We also have no matched drawbar, fuel-use or whole-season ownership test against an Ursa tractor.

DEFINE THE JOB.

Different power classes do not establish interchangeable machines.

Ursa’s different proposition starts in Bowden

Ursa’s own site says its tractors are assembled in Bowden, Alberta, and describes mechanical-diesel front-wheel-assist classes of 150, 180 and 260 hp. It lists 5.9 L engines for the first two classes and 8.3 L for the 260 class. Crucially, that same page calls these “concept specifications,” labels displayed prices as samples and asks buyers to confirm final specifications and quotes. This article therefore treats them as a discussion lineup, not a binding offer or proof of current availability.

Greg Berg’s Alberta Farmer Express reporting describes the company’s low-tech, repair-focused approach. That is useful context for the ownership question, not evidence that every owner can fix every fault or that parts will always be available. We have not inspected an Ursa tractor, audited production, confirmed a particular sale or tested repair costs. “Assembled in Alberta” also does not establish that every component was manufactured in Canada.

Compare the implement and season before comparing the badge

A farm considering a very large articulated machine should first define the implements, work rate, field access and timing it needs. A business exploring a 150–260 hp front-wheel-assist class should do the same at its own scale. Neither specification list establishes whether the machine suits a particular seeder, loader, transport route or operator. A stated maximum hydraulic flow is only one part of an implement-compatibility discussion.

Ask each supplier to work through the actual job with you. Which model, configuration and supported implement combination is being quoted? What are the operating limits and the expected delivery arrangements? Are the figures peak or continuous, and what evidence supports the claimed performance? The useful comparison is the complete proposed system doing an agreed job, rather than a horsepower ratio between machines aimed at different workloads.

CHECK THE SUPPORT.

Mechanical and hybrid systems both need a credible repair path.

Simplicity and sophistication both need a support plan

Mechanical simplicity can be an attractive design objective when an owner values understandable service access and local repair relationships. It does not remove wear, eliminate the need for manuals or guarantee an affordable replacement part. Ask Ursa which parts, documentation and repair support apply to the exact configuration, and have the arrangements set out clearly before relying on them.

A hybrid architecture introduces a different support conversation. Ask Zoomlion or its verified representative who is trained and authorised to assess that model, what diagnostic access and parts support exist in the intended market, and how the machine is handled when specialist attention is needed. Do not assume that a general hydraulic workshop can diagnose an 800 V hybrid powertrain. This is a support-planning question, not an instruction to open or service a high-voltage system.

Write down the costs that the launch cannot settle

A purchase comparison needs a final written equipment scope, delivered cost, financing terms where relevant, scheduled service requirements, consumables, transport, training and a credible response to downtime. We cannot fill those fields from a show announcement and sample website prices. This article makes no claim that the larger hybrid is cheaper per hectare, that the mechanical machine is cheaper over its life, or that either has proven superior fuel economy in the reader’s operation.

Use a simple ownership worksheet with three columns: what the supplier promises in writing, what independent evidence you have, and what remains unresolved. Keep a claimed efficiency improvement separate from a measured result under comparable conditions. A neighbour’s positive experience can start a question; it cannot establish the outcome for another configuration, workload or support network.

SEPARATE CLAIMS FROM PROOF.

Write down what is confirmed and what remains unanswered.

Kong’s view: choose a repair path as carefully as a power class

The interesting industry story is not that one philosophy has defeated the other. It is that buyers are being offered very different answers to the same business concern: keep the machine productive and make the next decision understandable. Sophisticated control may be relevant to a large integrated operation. A mechanically focused layout may be relevant to another owner’s priorities. The case has to be made around the actual work and the available support.

For broader context, read Kong’s existing Chinese-machinery-in-Canada analysis and its repair-choice editorial. Both return to the practical issue behind the headline: who supports the machine when the next question is about a fault, a part or an implement rather than a brochure figure? Ask for a clear answer before treating either high technology or low complexity as the complete ownership plan.
