John Deere 9RX autonomy: big tracks, precise questions

Kong’s ownership notebook: separate the autonomous tillage reveal from the exact machine, implement, supervision and support package.

By Kong Journal · October 30, 2026 · Sources checked October 30, 2026

Big tracks catch the eye. The more interesting autonomy question is what happens around them: who prepares the job, which implement is supported and who responds when work stops? John Deere’s 9RX tillage reveal provides a useful starting point for that conversation. Kong’s pick today is the ownership question behind the technology, rather than a claim that the biggest machine is the right machine for every farm.

What Deere actually announced

In its January 6, 2025 CES release, Deere described a 9RX application for large-scale tillage using its second-generation autonomy kit. The company identified 16 cameras arranged in pods for a surrounding view. It also described remote machine management through Operations Center Mobile, including images, live video and notifications about job-quality or machine-health issues. These are manufacturer descriptions from that announcement, not functions tested by Kong. [1]

The release distinguished selected factory autonomy-ready machines from retrofit paths for certain existing machines. It did not make every 9RX, implement or task autonomous. This October 30 article revisits that earlier reveal; it does not announce a new launch or confirm Canadian sales, delivery, supported combinations or current subscription terms. Those details need current written confirmation for the proposed package.

Start with the work, then identify the package

Before comparing autonomy offers, write a short description of the actual job: the field, intended tillage operation, implement identity and the people responsible for preparation and response. Ask a verified authorised supplier to identify the exact tractor, model year, installed equipment and supported implement. Keep that answer attached to the proposal. A family name on a bonnet is not a configuration record.

Request a demonstration plan tied to your proposed work. Which functions will it demonstrate? What conditions are excluded? What training, connectivity and support arrangements are required? An impressive demonstration can still leave a different field or implement unanswered. These are Kong’s suggested purchasing questions, not operating instructions or a statement that a particular combination is approved.

Remote oversight still needs a named person

A phone view changes where information is received. It does not answer who is responsible for seeing it and arranging the next safe step. Ask the supplier to explain the documented supervision requirements, alert handling and response arrangements for the actual system. Clarify how loss of communication, an interrupted job or a machine issue is handled under the applicable instructions.

For a hypothetical two-person operation, make the handover explicit: who prepares the work, who monitors the agreed information and who can obtain qualified assistance? Do not assume one person can supervise unlimited machines or safely leave a site. Let the manufacturer’s current instructions and the agreed operating plan establish the boundaries.

Put maintainability beside the technology discussion

Ask how the supplier identifies software versions, scheduled service, diagnostic access and replacement components. Clarify who handles an autonomy-system issue and who handles the underlying tractor or implement. Keep service records and equipment identification together so the next provider receives a useful description rather than an unsupported diagnosis.

The Kong takeaway

The useful innovation is worth understanding without inflating its scope. Compare the complete proposed work package, the person responsible for oversight and the support route when something needs attention. Leave the conversation with written answers and clearly marked unknowns. Kong has not measured productivity, savings or reliability, and makes no universal best-tractor award.
