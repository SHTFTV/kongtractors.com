This Kong checklist brings together manufacturer maintenance guidance and practical booking questions. Use it to prepare a useful service enquiry. Model-specific procedures come from the applicable operator and service manuals.

1. Start with the winter basics

Before buying expensive components, check the service schedule and record what is due. Battery condition and connections, coolant, specified lubricants, fuel arrangements, tires, lights and visible wear all belong on the preparation list. Kubota’s winter guidance covers these systems; the specifications and intervals must come from your own machine’s manual.[1]

A machine doing winter work needs an operating-readiness check. One being parked until spring needs its manufacturer’s storage procedure. Tell the shop which applies, including the attachments you intend to use.

2. Respond to regeneration warnings on equipped tractors

Not every tractor has a diesel particulate filter. If yours does, know what its regeneration lights mean. Yanmar explains that unnecessary idling can contribute to regeneration difficulties and that ignored requests can progress to restricted operation. Follow the instructions for the actual model; do not copy a generic button sequence from another tractor.[2]

Record the warning, operating hours and what the machine was doing. A warning does not establish that the filter or a sensor needs replacement. Ask who can perform the required diagnosis and whether manufacturer-specific equipment or dealer involvement is needed.

3. Bring the symptom—not just a guessed part

A pressure warning or intermittent electrical problem is a reason to gather information. Photograph the complete display message and note whether it appears during starting, under load, after warming up or only occasionally. Include recent repairs and any change in behaviour.

Do not treat a code copied from a different model as proof that your high-pressure fuel pump or wiring harness has failed. Ask for a diagnosis against the correct service information before ordering an assembly. Leave pressurised fuel-system testing and repairs to someone equipped for that work.

4. Put hydraulic concerns on the booking list

Visible hose damage, leakage or a loader that moves differently deserve a clear description. Does the attachment move slowly, jerk, drift or change behaviour once the machine is warm? Photos and a short account of the conditions help explain the request to Full Flow.

Never use your hand to search for a pressurised hydraulic leak. Escaping fluid can penetrate skin. Secure and isolate the equipment using its manufacturer’s instructions before inspection; hydraulic repairs require the appropriate procedures and equipment.[3]

Tell Durand whether the enquiry concerns hoses, cylinders, mechanical repairs or fabrication. Confirm the actual scope, appointment and parts arrangements directly with the shop.

5. Investigate new noises and driveline changes

Make a note of new vibration, unusual noise, visible leakage or a change in steering. Say when it occurs and which function is involved. Do not repeatedly load the machine to reproduce a fault that could cause further damage.

Gearboxes, axle housings and differential assemblies are substantial purchases. Confirm the fault, component specification and repair options before committing to a replacement. A useful service conversation starts with the machine’s identity and symptoms, not an assumption that a particular brand has a seasonal defect.

6. Order confirmed parts ahead of the job

Advance ordering is most useful when the need is established. Scheduled filters, belts and confirmed wear items can be sensible planning purchases. An unconfirmed pump, sensor or complete assembly can become an expensive guess.

  • Identify the tractor: make, model, serial number and operating hours.
  • Identify the engine and attachment: record their own identification details where applicable.
  • Show the component: photograph the part and readable identification plate; supply the existing part number if available.
  • Explain the job: symptoms, requested maintenance and the date the machine needs to return to work.
  • Confirm the order: fitment, OEM or aftermarket specification, availability, delivery timing and return terms.

Ask whether Full Flow will source the parts or wants to assess the machine first. Do not assume that a customer-supplied component can be fitted without checking with the shop.

Parking until spring? Make that a separate plan.

Storage is not the same as working through winter. Mahindra’s winterisation guidance discusses cold-weather preparation and storage, including fuel and battery arrangements. Use the procedure for your model rather than mixing instructions from different equipment.[4]

Keep a written list of deferred work and the parts already identified. Before spring, revisit the list, confirm the machine’s condition and arrange the required service before its first demanding job.

Winter tractor maintenance: quick answers

Should I order tractor parts before winter?

Order scheduled service items and confirmed wear parts once fitment is checked. Ask the shop to assess an unresolved fault before buying expensive components.

Does a regeneration warning mean the DPF has failed?

No. Follow the procedure for the equipped tractor and arrange diagnosis when required. A warning alone does not confirm a failed filter or sensor.
